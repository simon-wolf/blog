We are heading into my second winter as a runner. Last year I mainly switched to indoor cycling and even some treadmill running at my gym. This year I'd like to continue to run outdoors. To avoid having to only run at lunchtimes I need to be safe when it is early in the morning, dusk or even dark and because I live in a village in the countryside I don't have a network of lit streets to run along. It's one, not very well lit, road through the village and then unlit lanes and trails. I need lights to see with and lights and reflective clothing to help me be seen.

## Being Able To See

### Some Theory

There is such a vast array of lights for runners that this whole area seems overwhelming at first. There are lights available across all price ranges, head-worn or chest-mounted, different lumens (the brightness the bulb delivers), different colour temperatures (warm to cold), wide beam or focused, with or without red light, a huge range of battery run times, beam distances, weights, waterproof ratings, and more.

Starting from a position of ignorance there are a few things I can largely ignore, or at least not focus on too much, and there are a few things I think I need or want.

I think I'd prefer head-worn over chest mounted but this is fairly academic if I also have a ... I can at least experience both types.

Red lights are really aimed at preserving night vision if you need to read a map or are with a group of other people. They are also useful in an emergency (some lights can automatically flash SOS in Morse Code) and they tend not to scare off wildlife as much as brighter, whiter lights do. None of these are a high priority for me at the moment.

I tend not to do massively long runs so a battery run time of a couple of hours will be enough to get me started. Similarly, this also probably means that a heavier light is not going to be a massive problem but since I don't need a long run time I'm not going to need a big, heavy battery pack either.

I'm guessing that a wide beam would be better for me than a narrower one. From reading about lights it seems that wider ones are less disorientating (you're not looking at a "funnel" of light with complete darkness on either side) and I probably don't need a very focused light since the trails I run on are not particularly technical (although some do have gnarly sections).

Winters in the UK can be wet and whilst I'm not planning on heading out in heavy rain having a light with a decent [waterproof rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_code) would be a good idea. This really means IPX4 but ideally IPX5. Similarly, because of fog and drizzle, a light which can switch between a brighter, whiter light and a softer, more yellow light would be good but not essential (softer light reflects less off fog and rain so can actually improve visibility).

A bit of research indicates that I should be looking for a light which can output around 300 lumens. And this is where things get a bit strange. As lights heat up they reduce the lumens they produce to stop the lights from overheating. The testing standards that most companies follow require that brightness only needs to me maintained for the first 30 to 120 seconds. After that the brightness can drop away dramatically and it doesn't matter. This information comes from a really good video by Jeff Pelletier called [The Truth About Headlamps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-AZ1eEl0c90).

A more accurate idea of how long and consistently a light can output lumens can be obtained from an output over time graph which will generally show an initial peak, then a plateau and then a gradual (or rapid!) decrease. An example graph for a [Fenix HM65R-T V.20](https://www.fenixlighting.com/products/fenix-hm65r-t-v2-rechargeable-headlamp?variant=41994762223670) is below:

_With all of the graphs pay attention to the scales. Some will publish lumens and some will publish a percentage output. Most will use a non-linear scale for time._


Jeff's video mentions that [Nitecore](https://nitecore.us/) publish graphs in the product manuals which are available on their website and [Fenix](https://www.fenixlighting.com/) publish them on their product pages. He also says that [Moonlight](https://moonlightmountaingear.com/) prioritises sustained output over peak brightness so their reported lumens and run times are realistic and whilst they don't publish their data they will provide it if you ask.

_As an aside, [Lumonite](https://www.lumonite.com) have also been recommended to me and they do publish output over time graphs on their website but sadly they won't ship to the UK and they seem not to have an official distributor here._

### My Decision

As mentioned at the start of this post, there are a lot of brands and then each tend to have a lot of products. I decided that the only sane way to tackle this was to focus on some brands and products I'd heard a lot about and seemed to be highly regarded.

_The Fenix HM65R-T is one such light but it seems to be largely unavailable in the UK with some suppliers listing it as discontinued._

My shortlist eventually settled on two lights:

#### Moonlight Noctia [[Link](https://moonlightmountaingear.com/collections/running/products/noctia-headlamp)]

* 500 lumens for 1 hour or 250 lumens for 2 hours 20 minutes
* 110g total weight
* IP68 waterproof rating
* 6400K colour temperature
* USB-C charging
* £117 approx. UK price

#### Nitecore UT27 MCT [[Link](https://www.nitecore.com/product/ut27)]

* 500 to 300 lumens for 2 hours or around 200 lumens for 5 or 6 hours
* 74g total weight
* IP66 waterproof rating
* Three color temperatures: 6,500K cool white light, 4,500K neutral white light, 3,000K warm light
* USB-C charging. Can also use AAA batteries
* £60 approx. UK price

At half the price, potentially better light output, less weight and different colour temperatures the Nitecode UT27 MCT was an obvious choice to try as my first running headtorch.
